Start with the basics

What is an enhancer?

An enhancer is a stretch of DNA that can increase the activity of a gene. It helps a cell control when, where, and how much that gene is used—rather like a volume control.

Why compare cells? Different cells have different combinations of regulatory proteins and different access to their DNA. The same enhancer can therefore have different effects. We study that question using measured activity in three cell types.

From a goal to a testable question

What we want to learn

Our proposed goal is to understand how enhancer activity depends on the cell, and how confidently we can trust the supporting measurements.

Enhancers help control when and where genes are expressed. We want to distinguish DNA fragments with activity shared across cellular settings from those that favor one setting, and investigate how reliable those patterns are. Our recommended first comparison uses published measurements in HepG2, K562, and WTC11 cells.

  1. Establish a trustworthy baseline. Check the data and reproduce a published comparison.
  2. Investigate a focused question. Assess a possible connection between cell selectivity and reliable activity evidence.
  3. Evaluate an original contribution. Establish what the existing papers have not answered, using prediction models if they help.

Our first deliverable: a documented activity table, clear figures, and explicit limitations. The specific original research question is still being refined; a new model or discovery is not an established outcome.

A recommended starting point

Why this study and these three cell lines?

We recommend Agarwal, Inoue and colleagues (Nature, 2025) because its experimental design fits our first question. The authors designed a shared collection of 60,000 DNA sequences for testing with lentiMPRA in all three cell lines.

  1. The same DNA in different cells. Matched fragments and the same overall assay method make the comparison easier to interpret. HepG2, K562, and WTC11 provide liver-related, blood-related, and stem-cell settings.
  2. Measured activity with a traceable source. Released design tables, measurements, and repeated experiments give us a concrete result to reproduce and check.
  3. A question that fits the data. Data volume helps us examine many sequences, but quality and comparability determine which measurements are usable.

Why not choose only by size? Frömel and colleagues tested 64,400 synthetic sequences. Their study could be a better starting point if we prioritize how DNA binding sites work together. The starting analysis should follow the question.

This is a proposed route. The 60,000 figure is the designed library size. We recovered 55,977 names in the published specificity table through barcode coverage, but have not reproduced its numerical scores. Selected laboratory models and DNA fragments limit generalization; reproducing cell specificity would reproduce an existing finding.
