Start with the basics

What is an enhancer?

An enhancer is a stretch of DNA that can increase the activity of a gene. It helps a cell control when, where, and how much that gene is used—rather like a volume control.

Position is only a clue. Enhancers can act over long distances, and the closest gene is not always their target. Many can also function when their DNA sequence is reversed. Experiments are needed to establish which gene is affected and in which cells.

Why compare cells? Different cells have different combinations of regulatory proteins and different access to their DNA. The same enhancer can therefore have different effects. Our planned comparison uses HepG2, K562, and WTC11 because Agarwal, Inoue et al. (Nature, 2025) tested the same DNA sequences in all three. This provides comparable measurements for checking our predictions.

Our updated direction

Reproduce first. Then connect prediction with experiment.

Reconstruct the original prediction methods, then ask whether their scores relate to experimentally measured enhancer activity in HepG2, K562, and WTC11.

We began with the papers and sequence files supplied by Prof. Dehzangi. Reconstructing these methods provides a documented starting point. We chose the three cell lines from the newer Agarwal, Inoue and colleagues study (Nature, 2025) because it tested the same DNA sequences in each one. That gives us comparable experimental measurements to check whether the reconstructed models’ predictions agree with activity in different cells.

The scientific purpose: learn whether higher model scores are associated with activity across these three cellular settings, or whether the relationship differs between them. This would help define where the models are useful and which aspects of enhancer activity they miss. Why the same DNA is compared across cells →

  1. Reproduce. Recover the original labels, rebuild iEnhancer-2L, and document agreement or differences from its published results.
  2. Evaluate fairly. Keep related sequences together and separate training, model selection, and final testing.
  3. Connect to new experiments. Compare the reproduced model’s scores with lentiMPRA activity in each cell, then consider retraining as a separate analysis.

From reproduction to experimental evaluation

Why connect to this study and these three cells?

Agarwal, Inoue and colleagues (Nature, 2025) tested a shared library of 60,000 DNA sequences with lentiMPRA in HepG2, K562, and WTC11. We chose these three because that study provides activity measurements for the same DNA in each cell line. This makes it possible to compare the predictions with experiments across different cellular settings. Their 230-base constructs contain a 200-base insert between two 15-base adapters, matching the length of the historical benchmark.

  1. Test usefulness beyond the original labels. Compare model scores with experimental reporter activity to ask whether patterns learned from the historical benchmark carry useful information into this new setting.
  2. Compare cells while keeping the DNA sequence fixed. The shared library lets us examine each adequately measured fragment in HepG2, K562, and WTC11. This can distinguish activity shared across these settings from activity that differs between them.
  3. Define the limits of sequence-based prediction. Compare the relationship between scores and activity in each cell type. A relationship that varies between cells would show that the score has different usefulness across these settings; it would not by itself explain the biological cause.

One sequence, one score per fixed model. Our reconstructed models do not take cell identity as an input, so each gives a DNA fragment the same score in all three cells. Comparing that score with each cell’s measurements tests its usefulness and limitations; it does not turn these models into cell-specific activity predictors.

Planned analysis: the published design, activity and replicate tables make the comparison traceable. Measurement normalization and sequence-independence checks must be resolved before interpreting transfer results. The comparison has not yet been completed.

The old strong/weak labels are chromatin annotations, not high/low lentiMPRA activity. Exclude training matches and related loci from independent testing. Selected cell models and reporter measurements do not represent every human cell or identify native target genes.
