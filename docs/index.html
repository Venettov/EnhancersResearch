Start with the basics

What is an enhancer?

An enhancer is a stretch of DNA that can increase the activity of a gene. It helps a cell control when, where, and how much that gene is used—rather like a volume control.

Position is only a clue. Enhancers can act over long distances, and the closest gene is not always their target. Many can also function when their DNA sequence is reversed. Experiments are needed to establish which gene is affected and in which cells.

Why compare cells? Different cells have different combinations of regulatory proteins and different access to their DNA. The same enhancer can therefore have different effects. We study that question using measured activity in three cell types.

Our updated direction

Reproduce first. Then connect prediction with experiment.

Reproduce published enhancer prediction methods, starting with iEnhancer-2L, then link their predictions to measured activity in HepG2, K562, and WTC11.

We begin with the papers and sequence files supplied by Prof. Dehzangi. Reconstructing a known method will establish what we can reproduce; the newer lentiMPRA study then lets us ask whether those predictions remain useful when activity is measured experimentally.

  1. Reproduce. Recover the original labels, rebuild iEnhancer-2L, and document agreement or differences from its published results.
  2. Evaluate fairly. Keep related sequences together and separate training, model selection, and final testing.
  3. Connect to new experiments. Compare the reproduced model’s scores with lentiMPRA activity in each cell, then consider retraining as a separate analysis.

Current stage: the paper review and label recovery are complete. Model training, reproduction of published accuracy, and the experimental transfer evaluation are still planned. lentiMPRA numerical specificity-score reproduction remains unresolved.

Materials supplied by Prof. Dehzangi

The original papers and their data

The four original papers are iEnhancer-2L, EnhancerPred, iEnhancer-EL, and MCSE-Enhancer. They largely share a historical benchmark, rather than providing four independent biological datasets.

We recovered the benchmark’s 742 strong, 742 weak, and 1,484 non-enhancer labels from Supplement S1. Supplement S2 supplies DNA property tables for iEnhancer-2L. These chromatin annotations are different from measured reporter activity.

Verified findings · 12 October 2026

What is established and what remains open

Original-data review: all 100 strong test enhancers match training sequences, also in the current publisher supplement and inspected MCSE code. Removing them leaves no strong test class. Two old training sequences also match inserts in the new lentiMPRA design.

New-data audit: all 109 resources are present locally (51.07 GB). Coverage recovers 55,977 specificity-table names, but the scores are not reproduced. Combined and replicate ENCODE files repeat measurements. The MCSE notebooks, downloader receipt handling, and archived DNABERT launcher need repair before their intended use.

From reproduction to experimental evaluation

Why connect to this study and these three cells?

Agarwal, Inoue and colleagues (Nature, 2025) designed 60,000 sequences for testing with lentiMPRA in all three cell lines. Their 230-base constructs contain a 200-base insert between two 15-base adapters, matching the length of the historical benchmark.

  1. A direct connection through sequence. The same feature calculations can be applied to the experimental inserts.
  2. Measured activity in different cells. HepG2, K562, and WTC11 provide distinct cellular settings for evaluating the predictions.
  3. A traceable comparison. Published design, activity, and replicate tables let us check the data before testing transfer.

The old strong/weak labels are chromatin annotations, not high/low lentiMPRA activity. Exclude training matches and related loci from independent testing. Selected cell models and reporter measurements do not represent every human cell or identify native target genes.
